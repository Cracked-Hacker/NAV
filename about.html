Why NAV exists

Why I started NAV.

I started NAV to follow my passion for fitness, and now I am building coaching and science education resources continue my passion for science and training during this time when many are afraid that AI will take their jobs - fear not, AI is awful at being a human coach, and it's not even great at being AI, it can make an AI slop program for sure, but that's all it is - AI slop, not a curated, carefully calculated and customized program made for exactly one individual with unique goals and a unique human body. AI misses the nuance and the subjective art of coaching. That is why coaches don't need to be afraid of losing their jobs to Chat gpt. Instead of being replaced by AI, use it as a tool, that's what it is - a tool, and nothing more. It can accelerate workflows, and power app functions that can reach more people - and that's how I used AI to help me make my app NAV Coach, it keeps the human coach in charge, and uses the power of AI to accelerate workflows, collect and track athlete workouts, and make calculations easier so that you can get back to doing what you love to do - coaching! NAV is short for many things, such as NAVigating to a healthier you. For me, it means Nate's Athletic Venture. What is your NAV? I personally use the app to track my own workouts, as I do for my clients. There are tons of little things to check out - even a calculator that can tell you how many eggs you just lifted! I won't spoil too much, just go try it out and have fun messing around with it! I hope you enjoy it; it has been more than a year of work in the making and counting. If you like it, let me know with a review! If you don't like it, even better - let me know in an email or in the feedback section of the app so I can fix it or add what may be missing for you. Thanks for checking out my website and my products and for considering supporting me as a coach, educator and a creator, thank you so much!

Coaching and athletics

Although I’m now a strength coach and competitive powerlifter, my athletic background spans a wide range of sports. Growing up, I swam competitively for several years across three swim teams, played basketball, soccer, and football, and competed in track and field events, including pole vault, shot put, and the 100-meter dash.

Of all these sports, lacrosse was my primary passion. I started playing in Michigan when I was in fourth grade and continued after moving to Washington during my junior year of high school. At Bothell High School, the third high school I attended, I became the school's first-ever starting faceoff specialist (FOGO) under Coach Cartwright. I later attended Western Washington University, where I continued playing lacrosse while pursuing my education. Being a Viking was about more than competing. It meant being part of a team, working toward common goals, and belonging to a community of athletes from different backgrounds. Some of my closest connections and most valuable lessons came from being part of that community.

But my relationship with fitness wasn't always straightforward.

Growing up, I struggled with my weight and experienced bullying. Moving frequently and adjusting to new schools presented their own challenges, but athletics gave me something I could work toward and improve through my own efforts. Before college, I lost 65 pounds. That transformation changed how I viewed myself and what I believed I was capable of accomplishing. Exercise became an important part of my life, not just for physical fitness, but for confidence, stress management, and the satisfaction of working toward something meaningful.

Alongside lacrosse, I developed a passion for weightlifting and powerlifting. I loved learning new movements, improving my technique, and seeing what my body could accomplish through consistent training. I eventually competed in both sports. My November 2026 résumé documents a 522.5 kg (approximately 1,152 lb) powerlifting total in competition and a 513 lb deadlift personal record outside competition. I continue training regularly, pursuing new strength goals, and learning from my own performance.

In one of my medical-school application essays, I wrote that the barbell had given me much more than a physique or muscular strength. I meant every word. Strength training taught me patience, mental endurance, and the importance of appreciating the process rather than becoming consumed by the outcome. Progress isn't always linear. Some days the weights move beautifully, and other days everything feels heavier than it should. I've learned to appreciate both.

The gym has also been one of my most reliable outlets for managing stress, especially while balancing academics, research, work, and athletics. I wrote something in that same essay that still means a lot to me: "I know that the barbell will always have my back."

My passion for lifting is ultimately one of the reasons I pursued degrees in the health sciences.

I entered college thinking I might become a marine biologist, but studying kinesiology drew me further into the science of the human body. I became fascinated by how muscles produce force, how the nervous system coordinates movement, how different tissues respond to mechanical loading, and what allows the human body to adapt to training. I wanted to understand the science behind the exercises I was performing, not just how to perform them.

I graduated from Western Washington University in 2023 with a B.S. in Kinesiology (pre-healthcare specialization), a B.A. in Biology, and a minor in Chemistry. During college, I balanced my coursework with undergraduate research, teaching, work as a nursing assistant, and participation in athletics. It wasn't always easy, but my experience with strength training had already taught me how much patience, consistency, and incremental progress matter when working toward a difficult goal. Those lessons carried directly into my academic life.

I also discovered how much I enjoy teaching. While taking anatomy and physiology, I asked and answered so many questions that one of my professors invited me to become an undergraduate teaching assistant. I happily accepted.

As a lab instructor, I gave interactive lectures, helped students through laboratory exercises, answered questions, and encouraged them to think through problems rather than simply memorize information. I especially enjoyed working with students who felt overwhelmed and helping them build confidence in their own understanding. I still remember how emotional I became when all the students in my lab section passed.

Teaching challenged me to explain difficult concepts in ways that made sense to someone encountering them for the first time. It also taught me the importance of humility. Sometimes I couldn't answer a question immediately, and I learned that being knowledgeable doesn't mean pretending to know everything. It means being willing to recognize what you don't know and continue learning.

I carry those lessons into coaching.

My coaching experience began before I became a professional personal trainer. From 2018 to 2019, I volunteered coaching youth lacrosse in the Bothell community, including third- and fourth-grade players. During breaks from college, I also returned to help coach my former high school team. I enjoyed sharing what I'd learned, watching younger athletes develop their skills, and contributing to the community that had helped me grow.

From September 2023 through June 2025, I continued volunteering as an athletic and powerlifting coach in gym communities around Bellingham. Working with other lifters gave me opportunities to share my experience with strength training while gaining a different perspective on the sport. Training myself was one thing; helping someone else understand and improve their own movement was another.

Today, I'm a personal trainer, strength coach, and group fitness instructor at Blinnergy in Woodinville, where I've worked since June 2025. I also work as a fitness trainer with EXOS at Google's Seattle South Lake Union campus, where I started in March 2026. As of November 2026, my résumé documented more than 1,300 coaching hours at Blinnergy and more than 700 hours at EXOS, and those totals continue to grow.

My experience in healthcare and rehabilitation has also shaped how I think about exercise. During the COVID-19 pandemic, I worked as a nursing assistant caring for people with a wide range of medical conditions and physical limitations. I later worked as a clinical aide at FYZICAL Therapy & Balance Centers, where I gained experience in a physical therapy setting and learned more about the recovery process following injuries and surgeries. I also spent time shadowing physical therapists.

These experiences became especially meaningful after I tore my hip labrum while powerlifting. Going through rehabilitation myself gave me a personal appreciation for the challenges of recovering from an injury while wanting to remain active and strong. It also reinforced my interest in understanding how musculoskeletal tissues respond to training, injury, and rehabilitation.

Today, my scientific interests include maximizing strength development in adults, muscular hypertrophy, connective-tissue adaptation, and the effects of resistance training on long-term physical function. I'm particularly interested in developing a better understanding of strength and hypertrophy training for individuals with joint hypermobility, including those with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS). These interests extend into my broader research goals involving musculoskeletal biology, cartilage regeneration, and maintaining physical function across the lifespan.

I want people to be able to continue doing the activities they love for as much of their lives as possible.

My philosophy as a coach is that training should be designed around the person doing it.

Two people might have similar goals, but that doesn't mean they should follow identical programs. Their anatomy, abilities, training histories, injury histories, schedules, preferences, and responses to exercise may be very different. Scientific evidence provides an essential foundation, but applying that evidence requires judgment, observation, communication, and a willingness to make adjustments.

I value measurable progress. Strength, repetitions, training volume, effort, and performance trends all provide information that can help guide decisions. But numbers aren't everything. How someone feels, how they move, how they're recovering, and what else is happening in their life matter too.

I'm not interested in giving someone a list of exercises and telling them to follow it blindly. I want people to understand their training: what we're working toward, why we've selected certain exercises, what adaptations we're trying to achieve, and how we'll evaluate their progress. I believe that helping someone develop their knowledge and confidence is just as valuable as helping them lift a heavier weight.

I also understand that people come to the gym for very different reasons. Some want to compete in strength sports or improve their athletic performance. Others want to build muscle, regain confidence, maintain their independence, or feel healthier and more capable in everyday life. I enjoy the challenge of understanding what matters to each individual and figuring out how training can help them work toward it.

I still consider myself a student of the human body. The more I learn, the more questions I have, and that's one of the things I love most about exercise science. Coaching allows me to combine my interests in athletics, science, teaching, and problem-solving while continuing to learn from the people I work with.

Strength training changed my life. It helped shape my education, my career, and who I am today. I hope that through coaching, I can help other people discover what they're capable of accomplishing, too.
