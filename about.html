Why NAV exists

Why I started NAV.

I started NAV to follow my passion for fitness, and now I am building coaching and science education resources continue my passion for science and training during this time when many are afraid that AI will take their jobs - fear not, AI is awful at being a human coach, and it's not even great at being AI, it can make an AI slop program for sure, but that's all it is - AI slop, not a curated, carefully calculated and customized program made for exactly one individual with unique goals and a unique human body. AI misses the nuance and the subjective art of coaching. That is why coaches don't need to be afraid of losing their jobs to Chat gpt. Instead of being replaced by AI, use it as a tool, that's what it is - a tool, and nothing more. It can accelerate workflows, and power app functions that can reach more people - and that's how I used AI to help me make my app NAV Coach, it keeps the human coach in charge, and uses the power of AI to accelerate workflows, collect and track athlete workouts, and make calculations easier so that you can get back to doing what you love to do - coaching! NAV is short for many things, such as NAVigating to a healthier you. For me, it means Nate's Athletic Venture. What is your NAV? I personally use the app to track my own workouts, as I do for my clients. There are tons of little things to check out - even a calculator that can tell you how many eggs you just lifted! I won't spoil too much, just go try it out and have fun messing around with it! I hope you enjoy it; it has been more than a year of work in the making and counting. If you like it, let me know with a review! If you don't like it, even better - let me know in an email or in the feedback section of the app so I can fix it or add what may be missing for you. Thanks for checking out my website and my products and for considering supporting me as a coach, educator and a creator, thank you so much!

Coaching and athletics

Although I’m now a strength coach and competitive powerlifter, my athletic background spans a wide range of sports. Growing up, I swam competitively for several years across three swim teams, played basketball, soccer, and football, and competed in track and field events, including pole vault, shot put, and the 100-meter dash. Of all these sports, lacrosse was my primary passion. I started playing in Michigan when I was in fourth grade and continued after moving to Washington. At Bothell High School, I became the cougar's first-ever starting faceoff specialist (FOGO) under Coach Cartwright. Lacrosse became an official school sport after I graduated, so I never got my letterman jacket for that – oh well, what can you do.

Read moreRead less

I later attended Western Washington University, where I continued playing lacrosse while pursuing my pre-medical education. This is when I was concurrently introduced to Olympic weightlifting and powerlifting as well. Being a Viking was about more than competing and burning the boats per say. As a Lax player It meant being part of a team, working toward common goals, and belonging to a special community. Some of my closest connections and most valuable lessons came from being part of the Western communities I found myself in. As a weightlifter and powerlifter, it meant developing more individual grit and confidence than ever before. It took time, hard work, and consistency to eventually reach a 225lb clean and jerk and a 500lb deadlift, I used to say “I’ll reach that goal, and then I can die”, but now that I have achieved those goals, I can’t help but want to go even further!

But my relationship with fitness wasn't always straightforward.

Growing up, I struggled with my weight and experienced bullying as many of you readers may be able to relate. Moving frequently (16 times as of today) and adjusting to new schools (3 elementary, 1 middle, 3 high schools, 1 university, and some other higher ed) presented their own challenges, but athletics gave me something I could work toward and improve through my own raw physical efforts. Before college, I lost 65 pounds. That transformation changed how I viewed myself and what I believed I was capable of accomplishing. Exercise became an important part of my life, not just for physical fitness, but for confidence, stress management, and the satisfaction of working toward something meaningful. Fitness not only changed me and my perspective, but it changed my passion and my college major too!

Alongside lacrosse, I developed a passion for weightlifting and powerlifting. I loved learning the 5 movements, improving my technique, and seeing what my body could accomplish through consistent training. I eventually competed in both sports. At the time of writing this, my olympic weightlifting total is 400lbs, ~181kg, and my powerlifting total exceeds 1207lbs, ~548kg and counting. I continue training regularly, pursuing new strength goals, and learning from my own performance, and my clients too.

In my medical-school application essays, I wrote about the barbell. The barbell has always been there for me. It had/has given me so much more than a physique or muscular strength. Maybe a med school essay wasn’t the appropriate place to spew on about the importance of the barbell as a figure in my life, but I meant every word. Strength training taught me patience, mental endurance, and the importance of appreciating the process even when sometimes becoming entranced by the goal being so close yet so far. Progress isn't always linear. Some days the weights move beautifully, and other days everything feels heavier than it should. I've learned to appreciate both, and they both hold valuable work and lessons!

The gym has also been one of my most reliable outlets for managing stress, especially while balancing academics, research, work, family matters, being a human in today’s world, and athletics. The barbell has always been there for me, and I know that the barbell will always have my back – and that’s why I know it will have yours too.

My passion for lifting is ultimately one of the reasons I pursued two and a half degrees in the health sciences.

I entered college thinking I might become a marine biologist, but studying kinesiology drew me further into the science of the human body. I became fascinated by how muscles produce force, how the nervous system coordinates movement, how different tissues respond to mechanical loading, and what allows the human body to adapt to training. I wanted to understand the science behind the exercises I was performing, not just how to perform them.

I graduated from Western Washington University in 2023 with a B.S. in Kinesiology (pre-healthcare specialization), a B.A. in Biology, and a minor in Chemistry. During college, I balanced my coursework with undergraduate research, teaching, work as a nursing assistant (including during the global pandemic), and participation in athletics. It wasn't always easy, but my experience with strength training had already taught me how much patience, consistency, and incremental progress matter when working toward a difficult goal. Those lessons carried directly into my academic life.

I also discovered how much I enjoy teaching. While taking anatomy and physiology, I asked and answered so many questions that one of my professors invited me to become an undergraduate teaching assistant. I happily accepted.

I especially enjoyed working with students who felt overwhelmed and helping them build confidence in their own understanding. I still remember how emotional I became when all the students in my lab section passed – every single one of them, keep in mind, when I took the class, half the class dropped out or failed.

Teaching challenged me to explain difficult concepts in ways that made sense to someone encountering them for the first time. It also taught me the importance of humility. Sometimes I couldn't answer a question immediately, and I learned that being knowledgeable doesn't mean pretending to know everything. It means being willing to recognize what you don't know and continue learning. A phrase that I later learned to value as a coach is “I don’t know, I’ll go find out!”

My coaching experience began before I became a professional personal trainer. In high school I volunteered coaching youth lacrosse in the Bothell community, including third- and fourth-grade players. During breaks from college, I also returned to help coach my former high school team. I enjoyed sharing what I'd learned, watching younger athletes develop their skills, and contributing to the community that had helped me grow.

From September 2023 through June 2025, I continued developing my coaching eye (for free) in gym communities around Bellingham. Working with other lifters gave me opportunities to share my experience with strength training while gaining a different perspective on the sport. Training myself was one thing; helping someone else understand and improve their own movement was another.

Today, I'm a personal trainer, strength coach, and group fitness instructor at Blinnergy in Woodinville, where I've worked since June 2025. I also work as a fitness trainer with EXOS at Google's Seattle South Lake Union campus, where I started in March 2026. I am always learning and I will continue to grow.

My experience in healthcare and rehabilitation has also shaped how I think about exercise. During the COVID-19 pandemic, I worked as a nursing assistant caring for people with a wide range of medical conditions and physical limitations. I later worked as a clinical aide at FYZICAL Therapy & Balance Centers, where I gained experience in a physical therapy setting and learned more about the recovery process following injuries and surgeries. I also spent time shadowing physical therapists and doctors.

These experiences became especially meaningful after I tore my hip labrum while training for powerlifting three days before I took the MCAT. Going through rehabilitation myself gave me a personal appreciation for the challenges of recovering from an injury while wanting to remain active and strong. It also reinforced my interest in understanding how musculoskeletal tissues respond to training, injury, and rehabilitation.

Today, my scientific interests include maximizing strength development in adults, muscular hypertrophy, connective-tissue adaptation, and the effects of resistance training on long-term physical function. I'm particularly interested in developing a better understanding of strength and hypertrophy training for individuals with joint hypermobility, including those with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS). These interests extend into my broader research goals involving musculoskeletal biology, cartilage regeneration, and maintaining physical function across the lifespan.

I want people to be able to continue doing the activities they love for as much of their lives as possible.

My philosophy as a coach is that training should be designed around the person doing it.

Two people might have similar goals, but that doesn't mean they should follow identical programs. Their anatomy, abilities, training histories, injury histories, schedules, preferences, and responses to exercise may be very different. Scientific evidence provides an essential foundation, but applying that evidence requires judgment, observation, communication, and a willingness to make adjustments. -*ehehehm - something AI cannot do.

I value measurable progress. Measurable strength, training volume, intensity, effort, and performance trends all provide information that can help guide decisions. But numbers aren't everything. How someone feels, how they move, how they're recovering, and what else is happening in their life matter too.

I'm not interested in giving someone a list of exercises and telling them to follow it blindly. I want people to understand their training: what we're working toward, why we've selected certain exercises, what adaptations we're trying to achieve, and how we'll evaluate their progress. I believe that helping someone develop their knowledge and confidence is just as valuable as helping them lift a heavier weight.

I also understand that people come to the gym for very different reasons. Some want to compete in strength sports or improve their athletic performance. Others want to build muscle, regain confidence, maintain their independence, or feel healthier and more capable in everyday life. I enjoy the challenge of understanding what matters to each individual and figuring out how training can help them work toward it.

I still consider myself a student. The more I learn, the more questions I have, and that's one of the things I love most about exercise science. Coaching allows me to combine my interests in athletics, science, teaching, and problem-solving while continuing to learn from the people I work with.

Strength training changed my life. It helped shape my education, my career, and who I am today. I hope that through coaching, I can help other people discover what they're capable of accomplishing, too.

Patient Care and Rehabilitation Settings

My days as a Certified Nursing Assistant often began at four in the morning with some quick yoga, push-ups, and a cold shower. Then I put on my heavily pocketed scrubs and bright orange hat and headed to North Cascades Health and Rehabilitation, where I worked while studying kinesiology and biology at Western Washington University.

It was 2021, and the COVID-19 pandemic had placed tremendous strain on long-term care facilities. Some days, I was responsible for nearly 30 residents at once, and my longest shift lasted 18 hours. With inadequate staffing and COVID-19 claiming lives at an alarming rate, the work could be physically and emotionally overwhelming. I watched body bags leave our facility far more often than anyone should have to.

Read moreRead less

There is one day I will never forget. I witnessed two people take their final breaths before my eyes. Afterward, I helped prepare their bodies for the morgue, gently cleaning them one last time, replacing their dentures when necessary, and respectfully placing them in body bags. I also comforted grieving family members who had just lost someone they loved.

But my responsibilities didn't stop when someone passed away. Other residents still depended on me, and I had to return to caring for them with the same compassion, patience, and reassuring smile, despite what I had just witnessed.

My daily responsibilities included helping residents with activities of daily living and monitoring their health. I routinely measured and documented blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, food intake, urine output, and continence. I cared for people living with dementia, cancer, amputations, cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, depression, serious infections, and other conditions, including those approaching the end of their lives.

The work was exhausting, but I loved my patients. I grew to know their personalities, routines, and all their quirks and muses. I carry the memories of those I cared for with humility, along with lessons that no anatomy or physiology textbook could have taught me.

I learned to remain calm under pressure, recognize when someone needed help, support my overwhelmed coworkers, and care for people at their most vulnerable. I worked like a dog, but I kept my head up and a smile on my face because I knew I was making a real difference. And despite the exhaustion, I could go home and sleep at night feeling like an exhausted hero.

After taking a little time off to finish college, I returned to nursing care through Staffers and Avalon Healthcare Solutions, working in skilled-nursing, acute-care, and long-term-care settings. Between these positions and North Cascades, I accumulated more than 1,600 hours of experience as a nursing assistant.

Throughout these experiences, I became increasingly aware of how much our physical abilities influence our everyday lives. I worked with people who depended on others for tasks that many of us rarely think about until we can no longer perform them independently. Caring for those individuals gave me a perspective on strength and mobility that has stayed with me throughout my athletic and coaching careers.

My experience working in physical therapy settings offered another perspective on the human body's remarkable capabilities, as well as its limitations.

While playing college lacrosse and competing in strength sports, I learned a lot about rehabilitation through my own injuries. My first internship came to me by an athletic trainer at WWU that had worked with me through more than one incident. She’d say, “what it is this time.” I eventually had the opportunity to shadow Lori for 100 hours. I watched young athletes progress through rehabilitation following surgeries, stress injuries, and acute injuries, and I even had opportunities to train some of them in the gym. I became familiar with cryotherapy cups, hydrocollators, goniometers, common rehab exercises and other rehabilitation equipment. During a campus health event, I volunteered to demonstrate ankle-dorsiflexion measurements and other skills I had learned.

In 2024, I joined FYZICAL Therapy & Balance Centers in Bellingham as a clinical aide, gaining more than 800 hours of experience in a clinical physical therapy setting.

FYZICAL felt like a family. I shadowed patient care, supported daily clinic operations, and learned about appointment scheduling, documentation, insurance billing, and the front and back-end office work that keeps the therapy work possible at scale. Many of the patients were recovering from major orthopedic procedures, including total hip and knee replacements, which gave me an opportunity to observe the rehabilitation process over time.

I also learned about how orthotics are made for people with different foot conditions, including complications associated with diabetes. One of my favorite experiences was participating as a test subject during a nerve-conduction study where I got to see my own action potentials! After years of studying physiology, getting to observe the electrical activity I had learned about in school associated with my own nervous system in real time was fascinating!

During this period, I was also experiencing rehabilitation from the patient's perspective. I had torn my hip labrum while powerlifting just three days before taking the MCAT, (which would later aid me in landing a medical school interview), and I received physical therapy for the injury at FYZICAL.

That also got me thinking about how my father and sister have also suffered serious spinal injuries. My father temporarily lost the ability to walk following his injury, but after rehab, he can still hang with ballers on the basketball court. These experiences brought the subjects I had spent years studying - anatomy, biomechanics, physiology, and tissue adaptation - much closer to home.

Together, my experiences as a student-athlete, patient, nursing assistant, and clinical aide helped shape my interest in anatomy, and musculoskeletal research. I became particularly interested in cartilage regeneration, connective-tissue adaptation, and the biological processes that govern injury and recovery, and furthermore – strength and hypertrophy. I hope to contribute to research that improves our ability to repair damaged tissues, restore mobility, and help people remain physically active throughout their lives, and without steroids and PEDs.

One of the biggest reasons I encourage people to exercise is that I have seen what life can look like when physical independence is lost.

I don't want people to wait until they are struggling with ordinary activities before they recognize the importance of their physical fitness. I want them to develop their strength, endurance, balance, and mobility while they can, and continue maintaining those abilities throughout the lifespan.

There are meaningful ways we can invest in our physical health throughout life, and I believe strength training is one of the best methods.

I love powerlifting. I love challenging myself, pushing my limits, and picking up heavy things. But the value of strength extends far beyond what someone can lift in a gym.

For one person, getting stronger might mean achieving a new personal record. For another, it might mean being able to get out of a chair, carry groceries, climb stairs, or continue enjoying a favorite activity without assistance. Neither accomplishment is insignificant.

My experience in long-term care showed me why those everyday abilities matter. My experience in rehabilitation showed me how much work recovering them can require. And my own athletic journey showed me how rewarding it can be to develop them in the first place.

I want to help people build the strength and physical capacity to enjoy their lives - not just now, but decades from now. If the work we do today helps someone remain active, capable, and independent for longer, that is worth far more to me than any number on a barbell. … but it’s still really cool to lift a lot of weight, and I hope you are inspired to do so.

Scientific Research and Publication

There were a couple of times during college when I actually slept in the chemistry lab. Between pursuing two bachelor's degrees and a chemistry minor, teaching anatomy, working as a nursing assistant during the pandemic, and competing in athletics, I had plenty on my plate. At one point, a tree even fell on my car. Life certainly wasn't boring!

From September 2021 through June 2023, I spent more than 300 hours conducting undergraduate research in Dr. Catherine Clark's environmental chemistry laboratory at Western Washington University.

Read moreRead less

Our primary research investigated how bacteria and dissolved organic matter influence the production and degradation of ethanol and acetaldehyde in estuarine waters. I worked closely with three lab partners, processing water samples collected under different conditions, including seasonal changes, sampling locations, and areas affected by wildfires.

My work involved preparing laboratory solutions such as DAPI and PBS, staining microscopy slides, counting bacteria, performing spectrophotometry, and analyzing absorbance and fluorescence measurements. I also worked with the fluorescence index, biological index, and humification index to help characterize dissolved organic matter.

One of my contributions was developing a method for quantifying bacterial abundance from microscope images. I derived a pixel-based conversion factor, developed an image-analysis workflow, and helped establish a laboratory protocol for counting bacteria in samples.

I spent long hours analyzing data, learning to code, developing simple tools for our research, and creating figures using Excel and OriginPro. I also taught myself redundancy analysis (RDA) to better understand relationships among the variables we were studying.

This was all while balancing a double major, a chemistry minor, undergraduate teaching, athletics, and my job as a nursing assistant during the COVID-19 pandemic. A tree even fell on my car, and I went through a breakup during this period. There were a couple of times I actually slept in the lab!

My research wasn't limited to the laboratory, either. I climbed a literal mountain to collect snow algae samples as part of our work.

Our findings showed that microbial communities and dissolved organic matter varied across the estuary, providing evidence that biological and environmental conditions influence the cycling of ethanol and acetaldehyde.

In March 2023, I traveled to Indianapolis and co-presented our research poster at the American Chemical Society National Meeting. I learned more about chemistry in a few days than I could have imagined possible. My undergraduate research also contributed to my receiving an award for academic excellence in chemistry.

I especially enjoyed applying mathematics to biology through quantitative image analysis, learning new laboratory techniques, and working with my labmates to solve problems.

After graduating, I continued expanding my research experience under Dr. Angelique Day at the University of Washington School of Social Work, where I contributed to a study examining how healthcare policy affects access to contraceptive care among young people in foster care.

Our team examined historical Michigan Medicaid claims data to investigate changes in contraceptive prescription-refill patterns during a transition from fee-for-service insurance to a health maintenance organization model.

I conducted literature reviews on contraceptive methods, effectiveness, and prescription frequency, and contributed to table development and manuscript preparation. I learned about the differences between short-acting and long-acting contraceptive methods, including the challenges frequent prescription refills can present for young people experiencing changes in their living arrangements.

Our analysis found that timely contraceptive refill claims became less frequent during the transition in insurance coverage. The study highlighted how healthcare policy can affect access to care, particularly for vulnerable populations.

Our work was published in the Child and Adolescent Social Work Journal in 2024, with me as a coauthor:

Claims for Contraceptive Care Among Female Youth Living in Foster Care by Type of Medicaid Insurance: An Analysis of Michigan's Medicaid Claims Data.

Working in environmental chemistry and social work research gave me experience with two very different scientific disciplines. I learned about the similarities and differences between research in the hard sciences and social sciences, and gained a greater appreciation for the role research can play in healthcare and public policy.

Today, my long-term research interests center on regenerative medicine, cartilage biology, connective-tissue adaptation, and the effects of strength training and injury on musculoskeletal tissues. I hope to contribute to the development of therapies that restore mobility and help people remain physically active throughout their lives.

Teaching and Community Science

Taking Anatomy and Physiology at Western Washington University gave me the skills to transition from being a good student into an enthusiastic scholar of the health sciences. I asked and answered so many questions in class that my professor eventually asked me to teach. I happily accepted!

In 2022, I became an unpaid undergraduate teaching assistant for BIOL 348 Anatomy & Physiology, working under Professors Gregory Freedman and Jaci Scheer. I spent more than 60 hours giving lectures, creating exams, and helping my peers through laboratory sections about one of my favorite subjects.

Read moreRead less

I usually began class with a PowerPoint presentation and a short interactive lecture, sometimes followed by a dissection demonstration. Students would then work in groups on their lab packets while I walked around answering questions and looking for anyone who seemed overwhelmed. I made a point of reminding those students that they were capable, and that I believed in them.

Rather than simply giving someone an answer, I encouraged students to think through questions aloud, work together, and develop confidence in their own understanding. I shared strategies for learning and tried to make difficult material easier to approach.

Being a lab instructor tested more than my knowledge of anatomy. I had to learn how to convey complicated information in a way that was palatable to fresh ears. Sometimes I couldn't answer a question off the top of my head, either. Those moments taught me the importance of recognizing my own ignorance and being willing to learn.

Many of my students told me that my enthusiasm for the subject was contagious. I will never forget the tears of joy I shed when every student in my lab section passed. Several of the teaching assistants who followed me came from that same section.

I also went on to become one of eight students at Western granted the opportunity to dissect human cadavers that year, further developing my understanding of human anatomy.

My interest in teaching science continued beyond college. I've worked on developing community biotechnology curriculum with Sound Bio Labs to help students in the community learn about biotechnology. I've also reviewed scientific literature and developed educational resources of my own.

Teaching anatomy helped me develop my leadership skills, but more importantly, it showed me how much I enjoy helping others learn. I discovered that teaching others is one of the best ways to solidify my own knowledge.

That experience continues to shape how I coach today.

I want people to understand their training: what we're working toward, why we've selected certain exercises, what adaptations we're trying to achieve, and how we'll evaluate their progress.

Just as I did in the anatomy lab, I want the people I work with to ask questions, think critically, develop confidence, and recognize what they're capable of accomplishing. I believe helping someone develop their knowledge and confidence is just as valuable as helping them lift a heavier weight.

I'm not a drill sergeant. I'm a teacher.
