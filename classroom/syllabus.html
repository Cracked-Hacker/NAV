Course syllabus

Integrated Science of Natural Strength, Hypertrophy, Power, and Lifelong Resilience. This is NAV's university-ready curriculum draft adapted for Classroom delivery; it is not a claim of institutional approval or accreditation.

10 instructional weeks4-quarter-credit proposal format~120–126 total student-work hoursNo capstone

Course reasoning loop

Assess → Explain → Prescribe → Monitor → Adjust → Refer

Scientific topics earn their place by improving one or more parts of this reasoning process. Students are graded on reasoning, analysis, evidence use, communication, safety, and scope—not athletic performance.

Catalog-style description

What this course integrates

Biomechanics, functional anatomy, neurophysiology, skeletal-muscle biology, connective-tissue adaptation, exercise physiology, nutrition, recovery, research appraisal, and program design are used to explain adaptation to resistance and conditioning exercise. The course applies scientific evidence to assessment, exercise selection, training prescription, monitoring, adjustment, safety, and referral through quizzes, applied laboratory work, and case-based assignments.

Course learning outcomes

By the end of the course, successful students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish strength, hypertrophy, power, rate of force development, work capacity, aerobic capacity, body composition, and tissue tolerance.
  2. Analyze resistance exercise using force, torque, moment arms, ROM, velocity, stability, anthropometry, and technique.
  3. Explain neural and muscular contributors to force production, including motor units, recruitment, rate coding, coordination, skill, architecture, and fatigue.
  4. Trace a defensible pathway from mechanical loading to tissue remodeling while separating mechanistic plausibility from long-term outcome evidence.
  5. Evaluate and prescribe load, reps, sets, frequency, rest, effort, proximity to failure, exercise selection, ROM, velocity intent, and progression.
  6. Distinguish programming priorities for hypertrophy, maximum strength, explosive power, conditioning, and mixed athletic goals.
  7. Explain major tendon, bone, cartilage, and skeletal-muscle adaptation principles and apply tissue-specific timescales without diagnosing injury.
  8. Integrate aerobic and anaerobic conditioning with resistance training using evidence-informed concurrent-training principles.
  9. Integrate energy intake, protein, carbohydrate, hydration, creatine, sleep, stress, and recovery monitoring within coaching scope.
  10. Interpret 1RM estimates, RPE/RIR, heart rate, repetition performance, body-mass trends, and session data while acknowledging measurement error.
  11. Critically appraise reviews, meta-analyses, randomized trials, observational evidence, mechanisms, and common fitness claims.
  12. Design, critique, monitor, and revise training prescriptions for defined goals and constraints using transparent reasoning.
  13. Communicate exercise-science concepts accurately without exaggerating certainty.
  14. Identify red flags, ethical boundaries, and situations requiring referral to appropriately licensed professionals.

Assessment

No single assessment dominates the course.

Completion is not the same as mastery. The university-format weighting below emphasizes repeated evidence of reasoning and application.

10%Retrieval / mastery checks
15%Weekly / cumulative quizzes
25%Solo gym laboratories
40%Applied assignments
10%Instructor-guided learning activities

NAV Classroom's current public progress controls are learning aids, not an official transcript or institutional gradebook.

Weekly workload

Designed around approximately 12–12.5 hours per week.

Instruction + core learning

~2.5 hours instructor-led seminar or equivalent substantive interaction plus ~3 hours of NAV Classroom modules/readings.

Laboratory work

~1.5–2 hours of applied lab work plus ~1.5 hours of preparation, data entry, and analysis.

Retrieval + quiz

~0.75 hour of quiz, retrieval, and spaced review.

Applied assignment

~2.5–3 hours to analyze, design, justify, critique, and communicate a defensible decision.

Solo-gym lab standard

Practical without requiring risky testing or research equipment.

Required design rules

  • One student can complete the lab without a partner.
  • A normal commercial or strength gym is sufficient.
  • No true 1RM, forced reps, blood sampling, EMG, force plates, metabolic carts, or unsafe failure testing are required.
  • Procedure, stopping rules, and facility constraints must be explicit.
  • Physical performance never determines the grade.

Accessible alternative

Every physical lab should have an analytically equivalent non-physical alternative using instructor-supplied data or video when participation is contraindicated or inaccessible. Students are assessed on observation, analysis, interpretation, and reasoning.

Evidence standard

Claims carry their confidence level.

A

High confidence

Convergent high-quality reviews/meta-analyses and/or directly relevant controlled outcome evidence.

B

Moderate confidence

Multiple relevant human studies with important limitations.

C

Emerging

Plausible, early, or mixed human evidence; not strong enough for universal prescription.

D

Mechanistic

Useful for explanation or hypothesis generation; insufficient alone to prove a superior long-term method.

X

Unsupported / refuted

A claim that should not be presented as established.

Recommended preparation

Who the university-format version is built for

Upper-division kinesiology/exercise-science students, advanced biology/pre-health students, aspiring coaches, and strength professionals. Introductory anatomy and physiology or instructor permission is recommended; prior resistance-training experience is useful but not required.

Scope

Education and coaching, not medical diagnosis.

The course teaches monitoring, training modification, safety, and referral. It does not authorize diagnosis, treatment of medical conditions, or representation as a licensed healthcare professional.
